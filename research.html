Research

One question runs through all of it: how can a group of drones deal with a drone that should not be there without destroying it?

A swarm that surrounds an intruder on all sides forms a visible barrier. It tells the operator that the airspace is restricted and encourages the drone to leave. Making that work means placing the drones evenly on a sphere around a moving target, steering them there with provable stability, and keeping them from colliding on the way. The fixed-wing work came first: building a drone and a simulator that can be trusted.

M.Sc. thesis

Cooperative Encirclement of Non-Cooperative Drones

2026 · M.Sc. in Electrical and Computer Engineering · NOVA FCT · adviser Bruno Guerreiro

The growing popularity of drones has been accompanied by a rising number of unintentional incursions into restricted airspace, with documented disruptions to airports and other critical infrastructure. This thesis proposes a non-destructive response: a swarm of drones that encircles an intruding, non-cooperative target and forms a visible barrier, signalling to its operator that the airspace is restricted and encouraging the target to leave. Here, non-cooperative means that the target shares no control objective with the pursuers; it is not modelled as an adversary.

Most existing encirclement methods are planar or define encirclement as convex containment. This work addresses the fully three-dimensional case, where agents must be distributed evenly around the target so that no direction is left uncovered. Three architectures are developed in increasing generality. First, a centralized approach places agents on the two-sphere using a Fibonacci lattice and steers them with a Lyapunov-based geometric controller, with a Mellinger controller for low-level tracking. Second, a decentralized approach lets agents estimate the target position by consensus over a spatial network and redistribute themselves through Lloyd's algorithm on Voronoi cells, with convex-hull and half-plane implementations. Third, the formation is generalized to arbitrary surface meshes, including a velocity-weighted variant and a barrier configuration, and trajectory generation is replaced by a Bernstein-polynomial model predictive control (MPC) with velocity, acceleration and collision constraints.

Simulations with up to 31 agents, static and moving targets, and the mid-flight loss of several agents show that the formations converge and reform around the target, and that inter-agent distances stay above a safety threshold for almost the whole simulation. Comparisons of point-distribution, path-planning and collision-avoidance methods are also reported. To the author's knowledge, this specific combination of surface-based formation generation, decentralized geometric redistribution and trajectory-level collision avoidance for a moving target has not been systematically studied in the reviewed literature.

Conference paper · 2026

Hierarchical Nonlinear Control for Swarm Encirclement Around a Moving Target UAV

F. Cavalheiro, B. J. Guerreiro · 17th APCA International Conference on Automatic Control and Soft Computing (CONTROLO 2026) · accepted

This paper presents a hierarchical inner-outer loop nonlinear control strategy using a geometric Lyapunov-based framework for the cooperative encirclement of a target by multiple unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) in three-dimensional space. The encirclement problem is formulated on a two-sphere with arbitrary radius and center, denoted Γ², by projecting agent positions from ℝ³ onto a spherical manifold centered at the target, being the unit two-sphere at the origin 𝕊² used for intermediate calculations. Desired encirclement configurations are generated via a point separation method on Γ², while stable convergence from ℝ³ to these points is guaranteed using a Lyapunov-based reduced-attitude control law inspired by geometric control on the two-sphere. Formal stability results are derived using Lyapunov analysis and the framework is validated in simulation using a nonlinear quadrotor model and controller. Results demonstrate stable encirclement and highlight the suitability of the proposed approach for three-dimensional multi-agent aerial systems.

In plain words
Each chaser drone's position relative to the target is split into a distance and a direction. The desired directions come from a Fibonacci lattice, which spreads any number of points evenly over a sphere. A geometric controller slides each drone's direction along the great circle towards its point, a potential-field safeguard pushes references apart when two drones get too close, and a radial law brings the distance to the desired radius, faster once the direction is right. A nonlinear inner loop turns this into thrust and torques for each quadrotor.
Result
With three drones the formation converges to an equilateral triangle; with thirty-one drones (465 pairs) the inter-agent distances stay above the safety threshold while the swarm surrounds a target that moves and then stops. The desired direction is almost globally asymptotically stable.
Code
MATLAB and Python simulations and a Crazyflie experiment (Lighthouse tracking), open source under AGPL.

Conference paper · 2025

Fixed-Wing UAV Simulation in PX4 and Gazebo: An AVL-Based Approach

F. S. Cavalheiro, B. J. Guerreiro · 2025 9th International Young Engineers Forum on Electrical and Computer Engineering (YEF-ECE) · published

This paper presents a methodology for accurately modeling and simulating fixed-wing UAVs in the PX4 and Gazebo environments using aerodynamic data derived from the Athena Vortex Lattice (AVL) method. We introduce an automated pipeline that extracts aerodynamic coefficients from a user-defined YAML file, executes AVL, and parses its output into a physics-compatible .sdf file for Gazebo. The system supports advanced configurations such as partial-span control surfaces and hinge positioning. Additionally, a two-way parameter synchronization framework bridges discrepancies between PX4 and Gazebo, ensuring simulation fidelity. This approach simplifies deployment of autonomous flight algorithms in simulation.

The aircraft
An EasyGlider 4 with a Pixhawk 6C Mini flight controller and a Raspberry Pi Zero 2W companion computer, in a 3D-printed component housing I modelled in Onshape.
The pipeline
Describe the wing in a YAML file, run AVL automatically, and get a Gazebo SDF model with the aerodynamic coefficients filled in. PX4 parameters are kept in sync between QGroundControl and Gazebo in both directions, so the simulated drone is configured like the real one.
Flights
Autonomous waypoint missions flown first in simulation and then with the real aircraft, and compared.
Software
PX4, Gazebo, MAVSDK, QGroundControl, AVL (M. Drela, MIT).
